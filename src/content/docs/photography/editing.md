The importance of editing varies greatly from photographer to photographer. For some, it is an essential part of the process, and for others, they never touch their images after pressing the shutter button. It is up to you where you lie on this spectrum, and there is no right or wrong way to go about it.

That said, don’t be afraid to edit your images, as this might be one of the key differentiators between your images and those of professionals. Most photographers do at least some editing, and it can really bring your image to life.

## Cropping

Leveling and rotating are very self-explanatory. Perhaps you were leaning to one side or accidentally tilted your camera up. Your software can fix this (to a point) and help recalibrate your image.

Cropping can be very important, or not that much at all. It determines your aspect ratio, as well as what you want to put in the image. One of the most important uses for cropping is to get rid of boundary distractions. Below is an example.

These are two variations of the same image I shot in San Francisco. The first one is uncropped as I shot it, while the right one is cropped. You’ll notice that cropping it centers the focus much more on the two distinctly colored houses in front, while also removing distractions in the corners, like the small streak of light at the bottom left and the branch and sun-facing building side at the top right.

Doing this makes the photo feel much more <b>intentional</b>. You generally want all of the elements in your image to be carefully placed there, without anything looking like it was accidentally left in the frame. Although this seems obvious for a random branch at the top right, it can also include many less obvious elements.  For example, say your image has a lot of contrast, with the center being a bright focal point. However, you have a large gap or two in some tree branches off in the corner. Light shines through these gaps, and suddenly your eye is taken away from the center of the image, where the viewer should be looking. You should crop or edit this out.

Another obvious situation in which you would want to crop is shooting wide landscapes. Below is a photo of the San Francisco skyline from across the bay. My camera’s native aspect ratio is 4:3, which would have resulted in a large amount of negative space in the sky and water. Although negative space <b>is not inherently bad</b>, it adds nothing to the image in this situation and was best left cropped out.

## Value

Value, corresponding to the V in HSV, refers to how bright or dark parts of your images are. This is the most important part of the editing process and overall look of your photo. You may see plenty of beautiful black and white images (refer to Ansel Adams!\*), but never images with a complete absence of light and dark variation. This is why I suggest that you always start with editing the values in your image. This is usually done mostly with this panel in Lightroom, under <b>Basic</b>:

<figure>
<img src="/images/notion/2ff7b3bf-66c3-802b-bfd4-e10edc8fd62a-image.png" width="540" height="178" alt="" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
</figure>

Blacks and whites refer to the very darkest and brightest parts of your image, while shadows and highlights refer to the general dark/bright pixels.

### Tone Curve

The Tone Curve is also an extremely powerful tool for editing your images, but it is much more complicated to use. Think of the base curve as the function y = x, where your horizontal axis represents the input brightness of each pixel, while the vertical axis is your output. The dark gray parts you see represent the <b>histogram</b>, which shows the distribution of pixels that are at each brightness level. If we leave it at y = x, then each pixel will be mapped to the same value, meaning no change.

You can see that the image has instantly come to life. What I did by raising the values on the right, and lowering the values on the left, was to lower the shadows and increase the highlights. This effectively boosts the contrast of the image, which was very flat before.

As you can see, this allows you to manually create a sort of look-up table that reads the brightness of a pixel, and then outputs a different brightness based on the curve you created. This is much more powerful than the simple 4 sliders we saw before and provides more fine-grained control, but is also harder to use.  You can also adjust the RGB channels with the Tone Curve, which gives you great control over color grading. However, it is <b>very</b> difficult to get a good look using these controls - you have to be very subtle with how you adjust your points.

\*In fact, even though he shot on film, Ansel Adams did a great amount of “editing” to his images through the process of dodging and burning. Editing is <i>not</i> cheating!

### Color Grading

This panel is a staple of editing and usually defines the general look of your image by adjusting the colors of the highlights, midtones, and shadows individually. By dragging the circle around, you can shift the colors of each channel to whatever you selected. The farther the circle is from the center, the stronger the effect.

### Color Mixer

The color mixer is a very powerful tool that allows you to adjust individual colors by hue, saturation, and luminance (brightness, or value). When used right, this can create very unique looks and let colors pop out.

## General Guidelines and Advice

Editing is very subjective, so the goal of this section is not to tell you exactly what to do, but to introduce some concepts that you can consider. If you disagree with something and prefer it your way, you should do what feels right to you.

### Do not “overcook” your images.

One piece of good advice is to “edit little, edit often.” If we’re stuck in a dark room behind a computer for hours, it is easy to start perceiving your photos inaccurately. It is always good to reference your original photo often and revisit them with fresh eyes.

### Play with the sliders!

We aren’t always able to visualize exactly what a slider or setting does. Playing around with them often lets you explore new perspectives and gradually develop a better sense of where you want to take your images.

### Look for inspiration.

As with all art forms, looking at other people’s art is key. Having a few photographers whose work you really like is a great way to see how to edit.

### Don’t always crank up the saturation.

It is a common mistake to want to make an image more “vibrant” by simply increasing the saturation of everything, which then leads to an image that looks artificial and overcooked. Sometimes, what an image needs is <i>less</i> saturation or brightness in the right spots.

### Make sure your white point and black point are correct.

White point and black point refer to how bright the whitest whites are, and how dark the blackest blacks are. It is common to have a white point that is too low, leaving your whole image feeling too dark. A good way to check this, if your software supports it, is to add a quick white frame around the image. This gives you a good reality check as to whether it feels too dark.

### Let your shadows be shadows. (and highlights)

While it may benefit some types of photography, creating an HDR look by pulling up your shadows and decreasing your highlights doesn’t always result in an appealing image. This is a common differentiator between phones and real cameras, where phone processing tries to brighten shadows and reduce highlights, while cameras let shadows stay dark and natural. This is a major factor in that artificial “phone look.”
