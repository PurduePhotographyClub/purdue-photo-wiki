  <section class="gear-section" id="first-questions">
    <p class="gear-kicker">Before recommendations</p>
    <h2>Answer These Before Looking at Models</h2>
    <p>
      The importance labels below are only rough guides. What matters depends on your standards, your budget, and what kind of photography you do. A sports photographer, a street photographer, and someone taking casual travel photos should not all buy for the same priorities.
    </p>
    <div class="gear-summary-grid" aria-label="Questions to answer before buying">
      <div>
        <span>Budget</span>
        <strong>What is the real ceiling?</strong>
        <p>Include lenses, batteries, memory cards, and a bag. A cheaper body with a good lens is often smarter than an expensive body with no money left.</p>
      </div>
      <div>
        <span>Subject</span>
        <strong>What do you shoot?</strong>
        <p>Sports and wildlife need autofocus and speed. Street and travel reward smaller kits. Portraits and events often care more about lenses and low light.</p>
      </div>
      <div>
        <span>Size</span>
        <strong>Will you carry it?</strong>
        <p>A smaller, lighter camera you actually bring out will beat a better camera that stays at home.</p>
      </div>
      <div>
        <span>Mount</span>
        <strong>What lenses can you grow into?</strong>
        <p>The lens mount decides your future lens choices. Research lens prices before choosing a body, especially with full-frame systems.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="gear-band" id="used-gear">
    <div>
      <p class="gear-kicker">Used gear</p>
      <h3>Used Is Often the Better Starting Point</h3>
      <p>
        Many cameras age well, and buying used can save a lot of money. Sites like eBay, MPB, and KEH are common places to compare used prices. Check condition notes, shutter count when available, return policy, included batteries/chargers, and whether the mount and sensor are clean.
      </p>
    </div>
    <div class="gear-checklist" aria-label="Used camera checklist">
      <div>
        <strong>Check the listing</strong>
        <p>Confirm the exact model, included accessories, cosmetic condition, and whether anything is marked as damaged.</p>
      </div>
      <div>
        <strong>Check the system</strong>
        <p>Look at lens prices before buying the body. A cheap body can become expensive if the lenses you need are rare or costly.</p>
      </div>
      <div>
        <strong>Check the return path</strong>
        <p>Used gear is less scary when there is a clear return policy and the seller has a good reputation.</p>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="gear-section" id="what-matters">
    <p class="gear-kicker">What should I look for?</p>
    <h2>Specs by Priority</h2>
    <div class="gear-priority-grid" aria-label="Camera buying priorities">
      <article class="gear-priority gear-priority--cost">
        <span>Above all</span>
        <h3>Cost</h3>
        <p>Cost decides everything. Some cameras are strictly better than others, but the right purchase is the one that fits your actual budget.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>Mount</h3>
        <p>Your mount decides what lenses you can use. Most brands have their own mount, and third-party lens support varies.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>DSLR or Mirrorless</h3>
        <p>DSLRs use an optical viewfinder with mirrors. Mirrorless cameras use electronic viewfinders and are more modern, though often more expensive.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>Autofocus</h3>
        <p>Basic autofocus is enough for still subjects. Sports, events, wildlife, and fast movement benefit heavily from stronger tracking.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>Sensor Size</h3>
        <p>Smaller sensors usually mean cheaper, smaller lenses. Larger sensors can help with low light and depth of field, but the system cost rises.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>IBIS</h3>
        <p>In-body image stabilization helps you use slower handheld shutter speeds. It helps camera shake, not subject motion.</p>
      </article>
      <article class="gear-priority">
        <span>Important</span>
        <h3>Size and Weight</h3>
        <p>For many people, lighter gear means more photos. Portability is a real feature, not just a convenience.</p>
      </article>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="gear-section gear-section--compact" id="nice-specialized">
    <div class="gear-two-column">
      <div>
        <p class="gear-kicker">Nice to have</p>
        <h2>Useful, But Not Always Decisive</h2>
        <div class="gear-mini-list" aria-label="Nice to have camera features">
          <div><strong>Screen type</strong><p>Fully articulating screens are flexible for video and awkward angles. Tilting screens are quick and convenient.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Battery life</strong><p>Always appreciated, but extra batteries are usually easy to bring.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Weather sealing</strong><p>Helpful for rain and dust, but the lens needs sealing too. Most cameras are not truly waterproof.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Viewfinder</strong><p>Some people use only the rear screen. A good finder helps with fast action, immersion, and bright daylight.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Ergonomics</strong><p>A camera that feels good is more enjoyable, and that makes you more likely to shoot.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Megapixels</strong><p>More is not automatically better. Most cameras have enough resolution unless you crop heavily, print large, or simply want higher fidelity files.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Dynamic range</strong><p>Especially useful for landscapes and harsh light where you may recover shadows and highlights from Raw files.</p></div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div>
        <p class="gear-kicker">Specialized</p>
        <h2>Specialized Use Only</h2>
        <div class="gear-mini-list" aria-label="Specialized camera features">
          <div><strong>Pixel-shift modes</strong><p>Combines shifted frames for more resolution, but works best with static scenes.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Buffer</strong><p>Important for sports and nature bursts, where the camera must hold images before writing to the card.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Rolling shutter</strong><p>Mostly a video and fast-action concern. If it affects your photography, you probably already know why.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Shooting rates</strong><p>High FPS matters for sports, wildlife, and other fast sequences. Most people do not need extreme burst rates.</p></div>
          <div><strong>Dual card slots</strong><p>Useful for professional redundancy. Not essential for most beginners.</p></div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>
